Montville is being asked whether large-scale data centers should be allowed to be built
here. We think the answer is no — and we're organizing to make that case to the Township
Committee.

## What Is a "Hyperscale" Data Center?

When a developer looks at open land in a town like ours, they may see the site for a
"data center." That term sounds clean and high-tech, but it hides what actually gets
built: a windowless, warehouse-scale complex packed with thousands of computers running
every hour of every day, surrounded by the substations, transformers, generator yards and
cooling equipment needed to keep them running.

It is not an office park and not a factory. Almost nobody works there. A hyperscale
facility can draw as much electricity as a small city, and the costs of feeding it land on
neighbors and on everyone else's utility bill. The profits leave town.

## Why Data Centers Are Bad for Montville

<div class="focus-accordion">
  <details class="focus-accordion-item" open>
    <summary>1. Every ratepayer helps pay for the power they demand</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        This is the impact most likely to reach your household, and it doesn't stop at the
        town line. Data centers bid into the same regional grid — PJM — that supplies all of
        New Jersey, and the cost of keeping that grid reliable is spread across every
        customer on it.
      </p>
      <p>
        According to <a href="https://jerseyvindicator.org/2026/07/20/the-vindicator-explains-data-centers-added-billions-to-the-cost-of-keeping-new-jerseys-electric-grid-reliable/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Monitoring Analytics</a>,
        the independent market monitor for PJM, data centers accounted for <strong>$6.3
        billion of the $16.4 billion</strong> in costs from PJM's most recent capacity
        auction — and <strong>$29.4 billion of $63.6 billion</strong> across the last four
        auctions. That most recent auction cleared at its <strong>maximum allowed price</strong>,
        while the grid still came up 6.8 gigawatts short of its own reliability target.
      </p>
      <p>
        New Jersey households already felt this. The statewide supply auction that took
        effect <strong>June 1, 2025</strong> raised average residential bills by roughly
        <strong>17% to 20%</strong> depending on utility territory — an increase the Board
        of Public Utilities attributed largely to PJM capacity costs.
      </p>
      <p>
        There's a second cost besides the power itself. Serving a load this size means
        building new high-voltage lines, substations and related infrastructure, and that
        construction is generally recovered from ratepayers too. Transmission projects can
        also require acquiring land or easements from private owners. Before anything is
        approved, the town should know what grid upgrades a proposal would trigger, who
        pays for them, and whose property they'd cross.
      </p>
      <p>
        We want to be straight with our neighbors about what the research does and doesn't
        show. A <a href="https://policylab.rutgers.edu/publication/are-data-centers-raising-your-electric-bill-mostly-not-yet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rutgers New Jersey State Policy Lab study</a>
        looked at 2014&ndash;2024 and found <em>no</em> robust evidence that existing data
        centers had already raised nearby residents' bills by much. But its own title is
        "Mostly Not. <em>Yet.</em>" — because that data predates the AI build-out. The
        facilities being proposed now are orders of magnitude larger, from hundreds of
        megawatts up to a gigawatt, and the transmission upgrades they require take years to
        show up on bills. The risk is ahead of us, not behind us.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>2. You can't drink data</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        Servers generate heat, and that heat has to go somewhere. Many large data centers
        cool themselves by evaporating water — millions of gallons a year at the biggest
        sites — and they need it most in the hottest, driest stretches of summer, exactly
        when our wells, aquifers and reservoirs are already under the most stress.
      </p>
      <p>
        How much water a specific facility would draw depends entirely on the cooling system
        it's designed around, which is why it matters that the town ask the question <em>before</em>
        anything is approved: What is the projected annual water draw? Where does it come
        from? What happens in a drought year, when residents are under watering
        restrictions?
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>3. Diesel backup generators and the air we breathe</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        A data center can't afford to go dark, so when grid power falters it switches to
        banks of on-site diesel generators — and those generators are also started and run
        regularly for testing. They emit nitrogen oxides and fine particulate matter
        (PM2.5), the pollutants most associated with asthma attacks, respiratory illness
        and cardiovascular harm.
      </p>
      <p>
        Researchers at Caltech and UC Riverside put numbers to this in
        <a href="https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/air-pollution-and-the-public-health-costs-of-ai" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"The Unpaid Toll: Quantifying the Public Health Impact of AI"</a>.
        Their projection for the United States by 2030: public health costs of about
        <strong>$20 billion per year</strong>, and as many as <strong>1,300 premature
        deaths per year</strong> (their estimated range is 940 to 1,590).
      </p>
      <p>
        The generator piece alone is significant. The same researchers estimate that backup
        generators at Northern Virginia's data centers already create regional public health
        costs of <strong>$190&ndash;260 million a year</strong> — and that if those
        generators ran at the maximum level their permits allow, the figure would be roughly
        ten times higher.
      </p>
      <p>
        The authors also found this pollution "disproportionally affects certain low-income
        communities," partly because of how close they are to the power plants and backup
        generators involved. And they make a point worth sitting with: a data center pays
        property taxes to the one county hosting it, but as co-author Shaolei Ren puts it,
        the health impact "travels across the whole country, so those other places are not
        compensated at all."
      </p>
      <p>
        Their recommendation is that operators be required to actually report the air
        pollution caused by their power consumption and their generators. That's a
        reasonable thing for Montville to ask for up front.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>4. Constant noise and light that never turn off</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        A data center is never idle. Cooling equipment, chillers and fans run around the
        clock, producing a persistent low-frequency hum that carries — the kind of noise that
        is difficult to muffle and difficult to escape inside your own home. Noise is among
        the most common complaints from residents living beside these facilities elsewhere.
      </p>
      <p>
        Any specific facility's noise depends on its equipment and setbacks, which is why
        the town should require a noise study measured against Montville's own ordinance
        limits at the property line — not a developer's assurance that it will be quiet.
      </p>
      <p>
        The same goes for light. These sites run high-intensity security lighting all night,
        which carries into surrounding neighborhoods, washes out the night sky and disrupts
        sleep for the people and wildlife nearby.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>5. Big tax breaks, very few local jobs</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        Data centers are construction-heavy and staff-light. The building phase brings
        trucks and temporary work, but a finished facility is run by a small crew of
        technicians — a fraction of the employment you'd get from nearly any other use of a
        comparable parcel.
      </p>
      <p>
        That's the trade to scrutinize: permanent changes to the landscape, traffic, noise
        and utility demand, in exchange for a handful of permanent positions.
      </p>
      <p>
        The arithmetic gets worse if tax incentives or abatements are on the table. Towns
        elsewhere have granted very large exemptions to attract these projects, and these
        deals are often negotiated quietly, sometimes under non-disclosure agreements, so
        residents learn the terms after they're settled. The community keeps the traffic,
        the infrastructure wear and the utility demand; the company takes the profit
        elsewhere. Every number in that trade — assessed value, abatement, projected
        payroll — should be public before anyone votes.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>6. It changes the character of the township permanently</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        These are not office parks. They are windowless industrial halls surrounded by
        substations, transformers, generator yards and security fencing, often several
        stories tall and lit through the night. Building them means clearing land and adding
        impervious surface, which pushes more stormwater runoff onto neighbors already
        dealing with it.
      </p>
      <p>
        And it is effectively irreversible. Once a facility is built and connected, no future
        Township Committee is going to undo it. The moment to decide is now, while it is
        still a question.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="focus-accordion-item">
    <summary>7. Zoning decided in advance beats fighting each application</summary>
    <div class="focus-accordion-body">
      <p>
        Where data centers are already a permitted use in a town's industrial or office
        zones, a developer may be entitled to build without ever making a public case for
        it, and residents find out late. Deciding the zoning question up front — before a
        specific application is on the table — is what keeps the decision in the community's
        hands.
      </p>
      <p>
        We're not lawyers or planners, and we'd encourage anyone who wants the specifics of
        Montville's current ordinances to get them from the Township directly. But the
        pattern across New Jersey is clear: the towns that acted early had choices, and the
        towns that waited had lawsuits.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>
</div>

</section>

## Montville Wouldn't Be Going First

More than two dozen New Jersey municipalities have already voted to ban or pause data
center development, and the reasons they gave are the same ones we're raising here — power
demand, water use, noise and quality of life.

  <div class="partner-org-card">

  <div class="partner-org-card">

  </div>
  <div class="partner-org-card">

  </div>

Others include Pemberton Township, Monroe Township, Millville, Andover Township and East
Greenwich. Beyond New Jersey, New York's governor signed a one-year moratorium on
large-scale data centers in July 2026.

As Warren Township Committeeman Daniel Croson put it, "no single town can solve these
issues alone." But every town that acts makes it easier for the next one.

And this is not a fringe position. [Gallup found](https://news.gallup.com/poll/709772/americans-oppose-data-centers-area.aspx)
that **seven in ten Americans oppose** building AI data centers in their local area, with
nearly half — 48% — *strongly* opposed, and majorities against them in every party group.
For comparison, in the same polling 53% opposed a local nuclear power plant. Americans are
more resistant to a data center next door than to a reactor.

</section>

## What We're Asking For

1. **Keep large-scale data centers out of Montville** — decided in the zoning code, not
   application by application.
2. **No decisions without public numbers.** Any proposal should disclose its projected
   electricity demand, annual water draw, noise modeling and any tax abatement sought,
   in public, before a vote.
3. **Residents in the room.** Township Committee meetings are where this gets decided.
   Showing up is the whole ballgame.

## The Bottom Line

No community should have to trade away its clean air, its quiet, its water and its
affordable utility bills to raise a tech company's margins. What we're asking for is
ordinary: transparency about what's proposed, fair utility rates, and a real say in how
land in our own township gets used.
